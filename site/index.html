Tracking down carousel stutter

This carousel changes the visible width of each card as it scrolls. The image inside keeps its size. The effect should move continuously, even during a slow drag or the last part of a release.

Several implementations instead pause, move in small steps, or shift again after reaching a resting position. Stuttering was observed on desktop as well as on iPhone and iPad. Later versions improved some interactions, but the result still depends on the implementation, device, and input.

The earlier versions and diagnostic experiments are preserved here for direct comparison. The goal is to keep the changing card widths while making slow dragging and settling look continuous.

The core symptom

Stepping as motion slows

The last part of a release can hold at one position across several frames, then advance by a small amount. Similar roughness was reported in earlier desktop implementations as well as on touch devices. The recording below is one iOS example of the original settling symptom, separate from the newer handoff failure.

Watch the right edge of the expanding card. Near the end it holds, then advances in small steps. In native-touch traces, the final one-CSS-pixel offset updates were 50–116 ms apart while JavaScript frame callbacks continued. That is evidence for one motion path, not a diagnosis of every implementation’s stutter.

A separate failure in Touch handoff

Experiment 3 tries to smooth release with a fractional clock. It can also compete with native scrolling: some traces show a 6–7 px correction after the custom animation finishes. At the first item, the recording below shows the whole card layer displaced far to the right. This regression is distinct from the small settling steps above.

The cropped clip shows the return from item 2 to item 1. The paired trace reports native overscroll reaching −275 CSS px, even though the first card’s assigned translation remains zero. Smooth style values alone do not establish smooth rendered motion.

Device results and limits

Earlier implementations also stuttered on desktop. Later desktop behavior improved, while touch settling remained rough in tests on iPhone 15, iPhone 13 Pro Max, and iPad Air (5th generation), across iOS Safari, Chrome, Firefox, and Brave tests. A MacBook Air M4 did not show the problem in the reported later test. These observations refer to different versions and input paths, not a controlled benchmark or a claim that desktop is unaffected.

Native scrolling and Touch handoff felt smoother in initial phone tests. Touch handoff also produced the overscroll failure shown above. Desktop mouse releases use a custom animation clock, while Baseline touch releases use native inertia and snapping; those paths are not a like-for-like platform comparison.

The touch traces support investigating sampled scroll offsets and competing motion owners. Earlier desktop stutter remains part of the scope; it has not been shown to share that exact cause. Neither passing desktop tests nor fractional style values establish smooth rendered motion.

How the comparison works

One variable at a time

The masked versions use a sticky visual track and update card masks from logical scroll positions. Native scrolling puts fixed-width cards in normal flow. Rounded clock deliberately removes fractional precision from custom settling. Touch handoff keeps native dragging and starts a custom settle at release.

The fixture uses static colors, an 8 px gap, 28 px corners, and a 300 ms MD3 standard easing curve. The artwork is inline SVG. There are no external fonts, frameworks, analytics, or required accounts.
